# Richard Abibon

## 9TH DEMONSTRATION OF THE THREE TWISTS OF THE MÖBIUS STRIP

Marie‑Laure Caussanel’s questions prompted me to write this new demonstration: let her be thanked here.

From crossing to twisting: two logics of writing the 3rd dimension; perspective and topology

The stake of this work is a representation of the 3rd dimension, and therefore of what never ceases not to be written, because in the two dimensions of a writing support there will never, really, be a 3rd dimension. This is precisely the question of the *dit‑mention* [said‑mention]: how, in theoretical writing, one mentions the said. This overlaps with the question Freud treated under the heading of double inscription in word‑representation and thing‑representation. Words, if pronounced, are not those that get written. Sound as such is not written: a representation of the sound is inscribed, and it is no longer the sound, even if, when remembered, it may appear as an appearance of sound. It becomes sound again only if one actually speaks it anew, and not virtually in one’s head. That is why there can be no psychoanalysis by writing.

The 3rd dimension can be written according to the laws of perspective. That it took centuries to establish these laws shows well that they are in no way natural. On the other hand, they have something real about them. These laws are unavoidable: impossible to do otherwise, unless one decides otherwise – like Picasso, for example, for aesthetic reasons. But then he does not claim to be painting a perspective picture.

These laws I can summarise as an artifice which is precisely that of representation – that is, of *representance* [représentance] [^1]: the function that creates representations – that is, what concerns us in psychoanalysis: how to obtain representations of what is at work in us without our knowing it. For producing a symptom is precisely that: giving a writing to something we do not know, or which we know with an unknown knowledge: we have no representation of it.

The artifice to which perspective resorts is the following: depth, that is, the front‑back dimension (the 3rd dimension) will be represented by a dimension already on the sheet of paper: the bottom‑top dimension. The latter will therefore be charged with representing itself and with representing the absent dimension, front‑back. In a painting, what is at the bottom is also in front, what is at the top is also at the back. There are two dimensions, but in the representation they are the same. Exactly as in a Möbius strip: there are two sides, but they are the same. There are two dimensions to a strip of paper L and l, and by joining them with a twist we suppress the difference L, l; there is now only one edge.

More precisely, it is a combinatorics of the dimension x (left‑right) and y (bottom‑top) that will give the illusion of the 3rd dimension: the oblique lines converging toward the vanishing point write depth by restoring the illusion of increasing smallness that renders its perceptual aspect. These obliques are written in geometry as y = a x, where a is the slope of the oblique line, in which we find Lacan’s *objet a*, which never ceases not to be written. This slope represents what is not representable as such. It is a writing of the aphorism: the word is not the Thing. It is its representation.

![](images/6ed2c8bb1a49337cafcdb6e3ff5657d1d8d25c71147508825076e24f261d5312.jpg)

The Möbius strip does not claim to give the illusion of perspective; it writes the 3rd dimension as representation (and not as such) by writing that an edge x has come into continuity with edge y to make a single one. It does this by means of the inclination a, which indicates the necessity of reversal (or twist), and by the interruptions of lines, which indicate where they continue underneath, ceasing to be writable. This cessation represents absence: absence of line, absence of the third dimension. In the same way that a pronounced signifier can sink into oblivion once pronounced, this writing represents repression.

I write “can sink” because repression is not fatal, even if it is permanent. Thus if we consider the two edges at the bottom of the Möbius strip: one of the lines is continuous and goes around the figure, encircling twice one of the zones – precisely the one that is both above and below:

![](images/260155076d3a2016c0ba75ebd3d562a74ecaa95dbb95f7c98cd3895bd57590be.jpg)

![](images/27257f1be8deb01289ccae5a4920d209eaae0b7b3beb6da3d89cb386f29c089c.jpg)

This line writes the totality of the Möbius strip, and better, its movement. This line, written at the bottom according to dimension x, combines by the right‑hand twist with dimension y, y = a x; indicating in what way dimension x cannot represent everything: there is something unrepresentable that seeks to be represented. This right‑hand twist writes the moment when speech ceases to be able to say everything, and yet says it nonetheless, by making a detour [tour] in a combinatorics with the 2nd dimension y. Thus the meaning of this speech is double, which this writing writes by coming to border the yellow zone twice. There we have the Freudian double inscription: the signifier ceases to be one‑dimensional – the dimension of the edge (blue) – but, traversing it twice, it creates surface; this yellow zone therefore represents a formation of the unconscious: dream, parapraxis, slip, symptom.

This is what is represented by a single twist:

But the work of analysis consists in returning to this to make what is underneath speak: this is what the following two twists (turning counter‑clockwise) bring to light. These two twists write the cut (which could not take place without the third) of interpretation. What never ceased not to be written has found a writing (therefore: has ceased not to be written) through a formation of the unconscious that never ceases to be written: this is the continuity written by that line going around the figure. There comes a moment when this formation ceases to be written: through interpretation, which will dissociate the two edge‑writings, and allow the pronunciation of the two signifiers (two edges), one of which (green) served as a cover for the Other (red):

![](images/aa080a1c7cbc378f136f447c4d763c23ce1b9dfec824be57bed4ccdcdce4c78e.jpg)

By the same token, what is revealed is the inclination (as we say: to have an inclination for someone, or a penchant) – the cause of desire. Where it turns out, after the event [après‑coup], that the yellow surface was its unknown representation. Being both above and below (under the green zone and on the red zone), it wrote the compromise that every formation of the unconscious is, between two contradictory tendencies of desire (both above and below): the symptomatic representation was therefore itself a representation of the *objet a*, the contradiction never ceasing not to be written in the logic of our world.

The flattening‑out, which makes writing possible, has allowed the two contradictory objects of desire to be brought to light – a green one and a red one – represented in speech by the two signifiers, green and red (left‑hand figure), which the three twists have allowed to be spoken.

This is what the canonical representation of the Möbius strip fails to write.

A crossing of strings is another mode of representing the twist. This time, the above‑below of the symptom is written by a double interruption of the writing. Passing underneath, the green line ceases to be written. Another line, yellow, passing above, does not cease to be written, until an oblique line, red – a compromise formation – allows it to cease to be written by coming back over it. Thus what never ceased not to be written finds a writing that, in a new speech, ceases to be written.

![](images/d0e8905d3075bd5e350ec6cc8f36bb0b0f203c571a78c3d86cc41bd3d6ebe789.jpg)

And the three blue lines that represent the folds?
![](images/e40239869321a3512f45e51051cea12b5809f2860c38ff54d964a2cf2469ca01.jpg)

They are necessary because the flattening‑out of a Möbius strip really writes these three lines. The 3rd dimension: z never ceases not to be written (as such) and ceases not to be written (a representation of it is written). This corner line represents the twist as such, that is, the 3rd dimension = z: really absent, it is represented by this line, which is the edge by which x becomes y. It represents the place where the surface has the function of an edge.

These lines are the topological modality of the third dimension, as opposed to perspective. We have just seen that in this representation of the Möbius strip, the “oblique line” modality does not represent the third dimension; it only contributes to it.

![](images/2fd6344f27e54eb6e60750ef2f1189b0f7fc032fa64e9b1efab55e683bab2d02.jpg)  
To join above with below, two twists must be added. As a result, in the writing, one is obliged to add two lines as well, which represent these twists as such – the place of passage from above to below, the place where it is the surface that borders the void.

### The canonical representation of the Möbius strip represents repression

This representation of the Möbius strip:

![](images/86ed0e442674a3017d7fdfdbdb2f9e964e4800be9cc1fa89eb384da9d208a1f7.jpg)

I have already written in what way it is unsatisfactory, because the flattening‑out, in reality, gives this, depending on whether all twists are in the same direction (right) or one is in the opposite direction (left):

![](images/c0a3113246ad7f8bc7cbcb09d563c85ee2d2204d8599295f422bcf0fbc8ac092.jpg)

![](images/c6468a3948d2ddc96a65ee92954b400894545b30f7016b8f138f5fc972102b31.jpg)

So the question arises, in the preceding representation: if it is a flattening‑out, why not represent the three folds? And if not, why represent one and only one? It is as if we were in the presence of a half‑flattening, whose logic one may question.

If we suppose a strip of paper that is not an extensible material, it is the flattening‑out with three twists that imposes itself, even for a supposedly spatial representation, as on the cover of all the issues of *Scilicet*:

![](images/57ddbcb036d30bc915e710baa844182b4ae69d3e565d03644ee49df10c222018.jpg)

But one might suppose that the Möbius strip is constructed from an already flattened strip, a crown cut out in this shape:

![](images/ebafea0d230f4e4b9a6c3ecf42be20688330667a11dd9abd16cf085fef048d25.jpg)

One could then make a cut like this

![](images/fd3d190b4bb043fdff76c6e7234073266e89777f589953d6addea72e6e5c7c02.jpg)

![](images/275e8dae9e0daf1bd0bd95648cd9ad2b1180f98e0ee627331a2612800c51bf4b.jpg)

Then glue the pieces back together with a twist, so as to put into continuity the two sides – the one we see and the hidden side. Thus, one might perhaps find justification for the one‑twist representation, the curvature without twist being already there, as in the canonical drawing so often used by Lacan and his followers.

![](images/63d7b404724bb21248bdd98332386c2ace1c55ab45d4a99cdbb1161eeb3b921e.jpg)

Alas for the traditional representation, when one proceeds thus, one then sees the strip wriggling on the table, rising up like a snake with two additional twists, and refusing to return to the table once the cut is taped. One must then observe that the creation of one twist has created two additional ones despite the already present curvature. This observation is confirmed by a flattening‑out that brings out the three twists as in the triangular model.

So let us try to save this model in yet another way.

The loop is obtained by relying on this principle of classical topology: objects are flexible. One can therefore flatten the two twists and thus obtain a loop. If this principle can prove fruitful in purely mathematical topology, it is not suitable for the theoretical writing of psychoanalysis. Indeed, for classical topology, a pierced sphere is a bowl, which is also a disk:

![](images/9f5c88ef3456f2e95f2165e9a9e611caeb77eaa80920f09ff26884b8b5dd6865.jpg)

For classical topology these three objects are equivalent. Yet, from the pierced sphere and the bowl to the disk, we pass however from 3 to 2 dimensions: that is, from the real to representation, or from speech (considering only the third said‑mention) to writing, that is, to memory. We thus lose that richness which consists in adopting a theoretical model that writes what happens when we put something into memory – which involves the loss of a dimension. Moreover, in this loss we find the fundamental psychoanalytic concept of castration. Putting into memory is indeed accepting that representations are not things: we remember a representation of the thing, we do not bring the thing itself before us. For us, in psychic dynamics, a disk is a representation of the sphere, it is not the sphere, even if pierced. The disk is the putting‑into‑memory of the sphere (possibly pierced).

Furthermore, is this operation of flattening by softening neutral? In my view, no, because it amounts to adding a certain amount of surface to the initial surface, which is not trivial. This amount of surface is even calculable.

Suppose a flexible surface strip joined into a cylinder, to simplify the problem. The top circle and the bottom circle have the same radius R.

![](images/0f938a83a56d03635729ec2a80435b755c36280d70635a1cc25f7ec6c803fecd.jpg)  
If L and l are the dimensions of the strip with which the cylinder was constructed, the flattening of this cylinder, that is, its pressing flat, amounts to increasing the radius of the bottom circle by a length l. The initial surface of the strip was L × l = S. That was also the surface of the cylinder. On the other hand, it is easy to see that the surface of the ring obtained by flattening will have a surface:  
$S' = \pi (R + l)^2 - \pi R^2 = \pi R^2 + 2\pi R l + \pi l^2 - \pi R^2 = 2\pi R l + \pi l^2$

Since 2πR = L, we have: $S' = L l + \pi l^2$

The increase in surface is therefore $\pi l^2$, i.e. the equivalent of the surface of a disk of radius l.

The same applies regardless of the manner of joining the ring; a twist does not change the radius R, nor the length l.

Instead of writing two twists – that is, writings of the 3rd dimension, theoretical writings of speech – we add surface: that is what repression does, by substituting a formation of the unconscious (symptom, dream = surface, writing) for a speech (3rd dimension = twist). This recalls the washer that Lacan added to the Möbius strip to form the cross‑cap. We see here that there is no need to go via the cross‑cap to write what we need to write in psychoanalysis. Which is fortunate, because the cross‑cap is a figure impossible to represent in 3‑space, and *a fortiori* in the 2‑space of writing.

This washer, insofar as it is melted into the surface obtained by flattening, is the object‑cause of desire, the *objet a* insofar as it is not recognised – a letter stolen within readable letters.

![](images/2868eadb5fa50d31f61bc64d057aa8c3fe400fdf4b6f243a551f2c83e3c3a5b6.jpg)

This would be a writing of the cross‑cap as an embedding in a surface. One reads in it the disorientation that the concept of cross‑cap seeks to write: one does not know whether what is written is an upper side or an underside; it is “one side”.

The three twists that I propose to write, like this:

![](images/26290f609b2dda5e8875290bb11fc5848a8686b14b3ec1535dd50b52e3d5fbfe.jpg)

…write the 3rd dimension – that is, the passage through speech, which never ceases not to be written as such, but which finds a written representation in the same way that we write in memory what we have heard. They write the dialectic that constantly swings between repression (right‑hand and top twist) and interpretation (left‑hand twist). For there is never one without the other, as Lacan recalls when he says: the more the discourse is interpreted, the more it is repressed.

Conversely, the canonical representation, if one wants to read it as a single twist, does not account for this dialectic. If one wants to read it as a single side, it does not account for the fact that beneath every manifest discourse there is a latent discourse, which is the very basis of the invention of psychoanalysis. It does not account for the fact that, in analysis, instead of sticking to the manifestation of discourse (a single side), one cuts it – if only by the end of the session – so as to be able to return to it at the following session, that is, after this new cut of the beginning of the session. Then, by coming back to the discourse of the previous session, one brings above what was below. Lacan: “if the analyst cuts discourse, it is to allow the advent of speech.”

If one sticks to a single side, then one should rather write the homogeneous Möbius strip: each side is above‑below. It therefore writes analysis as directed paranoia (or two‑person autism), to be read as: one sticks to what is said. Nonetheless, Lacan said, one can emerge from this autism through speech, and that is the passage to the heterogeneous, by means of a cut and a change in the direction of the twist.

![](images/e234af32ba7d90f1aa9dd28fefbe74bd5cc21e451d6f579fc2d5f624537a6d7b.jpg)

Whether one reads it by cut or twist or reversal, interpretation is always what makes another side appear. The interest, however, does not reside in this other side as such, but in the dialectical movement thus initiated. This displaces attention from the surface to the edge, always in movement between one side and the other. This is no doubt what the proponents of the canonical writing of the Möbius strip want to read. If they read it thus, I find myself in agreement with them. But it happens that, in the reality of their writing, instead of adding more on the cut, they have actually added a surface of dimension πl². This is what the symptom does, or any other formation of the unconscious. This is perhaps not the best way to emphasise the cut, especially when one does not notice this addition, which closely resembles what happens in repression: what I cannot say (by a triple twist) I write (by a surface: slip, dream, parapraxis, symptom).

### The Möbius strip represents the interpretative cut

The representation I propose does nothing other than give the potential structure of the Möbius strip, when the cut is operated twice around:

![](images/6c6cc800f12d1c999edbf8af956b72df81a49f797428e2d249c3ddb959e5d3a5.jpg)

In short, flattening is an operation that accounts for the cut twice around. It accounts for what the uncut Möbius strip is, as a remainder of the cut – which appears in the yellow zone of the three‑twist writing – while the two‑sided strip obtained by the cut appears in the other part of this same writing.

On the other hand, when I was presenting this theory at Marie‑Laure Caussanel’s on 20 November 2004, Claude Harder opposed me with the usual arguments of those who remain supporters of the canonical one‑twist representation. Among others: “the twist is everywhere, it is not localised at the place where the fold is written.” I agree with this argument, however: certainly, the twist, as a function, is not localised, since no function is – being global by definition. But no function can be known without passing through localisations in terms of objects.

Now the very structure of this function, as such (and without passing through objects), is triple; I have already shown this long ago, by pointing out the following: what does this function operate? It operates the passage from one side to another side; in this sense, any edge noted thus:

…represents just as well this “twist which is everywhere”.

It suffices to add the two sides whose communication it ensures, to specify that this line is an edge and not something else:

![](images/bf1eee4391143e7276ac8e0d32264e650b2bc5b6094354e64790681461924950.jpg)

This line representing the cut between two sides therefore represents just as well this function as the Möbius strip represents it in its own way.

I take up the demonstration I had written for my first book on autism:

If one has an infinite rectilinear cut, cutting an equally infinite plane, it cuts nothing at all, because it never reaches the infinity of the edge. One would need an edge to that infinity so that the cut, meeting another cut, could cut out something – that is, a washer, a piece of surface; and even that is not enough, at least a third cut is needed, because otherwise nothing detaches:

Not possible to detach a piece

![](images/eae9ad716c7e0526c24ecf57cc84d8122edc845bbabba2486f53ec039163efef.jpg)

Not possible to detach a piece

It is possible to detach a piece

And one can then observe that the operation leaves a remainder: the initial surface pierced.

And this piece has Another side!

![](images/5348f79947c0af2ac06b41910f4778ffa49a2b7156377745b7da0433e2257bee.jpg)

This is why I had named “*acoupure*” [pre‑cut] that solitary cut which is incapable of producing an object, that is, a detached piece. It would be identical to the Möbius strip written with a single twist. I had proposed it as a model for autism.

One can also suppose that the solitary *acoupure* curves around: then it would not need two other cuts. But for it to curve, it must add to the first dimension, x, another dimension y:

![](images/000c92918bbd9e67ba7f0264b3a586248d0fbd67168c0f181ae3a7c5ac938477.jpg)

At each point of the curve, the dimension x is weighted by a certain function of y, the equation of the tangent at each point being $y = f(x) = a x$, where we find our *objet a*, cause of desire – the one that translates a penchant… but that is not enough: there exist open curves, like the parabola and the hyperbola, which cut out no piece in a surface.

For a curve to cut out a piece of surface, it must cross itself – that is, pass twice at the same place, writing an above‑below: the second cut passes over the first, detaching a piece. This third condition can itself be written in terms of function: $z = h(x, y) = h(f(x))$. Function f: the *acoupure*; function h: the curvature; function z: the re‑crossing. Three functions are necessary to make the *acoupure* efficient – that is, to turn it into a cut.

![](images/d0607d3db07c2905d775c04900318fecdf0cae9f19d6d853e44f73f6e0289fae.jpg)  
What the Möbius strip writes with its three twists, identical to the three necessary functions

![](images/0a8cb2e8ce63b5d6048ec97e47487b4ffbff59296cb76a5c5850773cc6184b31.jpg)

Obviously the function z is everywhere: the Möbius strip writes in its entirety the very fact of passing into the third dimension – the fact of turning the page or turning over the washer to discover its Other side. It is a writing of the third dimension; and yet writing cannot be the third dimension as such, since it is situated in the two dimensions of the page. It is therefore a theoretical writing of what never ceases not to be written: a writing of enunciation insofar as it cannot do without the support of the voice. The analysis of the concept of the third dimension brings us back to this obvious fact: the third dimension presupposes the other two. No three without two or one. The three‑twist writing writes the act of the cut as well as its product: two sides and a remainder.

Thus the third dimension is written on the third twist – the one that is not in the same direction as the other two, the one that, by making the difference, ensures the cut. This one, in act, articulates one side with the Other at the level of a fold, while opposite is inscribed the yellow side that writes the *objet a*, also in the third dimension, but in a passive manner. This surface indeed writes the passage from one side to the other, but in a disoriented way: it disorients (that is repression: the addition of surface that makes the cut here written with surface – the yellow zone) whereas the articulation of the third twist orients (that is interpretation).

Consequently, whatever the chosen mode of writing – rectilinear cut, curved cut, Möbius strip – one cannot avoid inscribing the three in the writing. Since the Möbius strip is precisely there as a concept to write what the structure of the cut is, writing it with a single twist would be to miss its function of representing the concept of the cut.

Here I join one of Claude Harder’s last arguments during that evening: he came to say that even the one‑twist writing was not suitable, indeed, since it was a matter of representing the Möbius strip “which is in the head” – that is, if I understood correctly, the concept: now the concept, however one approaches it, is triple. Likewise, there is no Borromean knot below three rings. The Borromean knot is nothing other than another writing of the cut.

The advantage of these demonstrations is to emphasise the fact that the One cannot stand alone, in accordance with Lacan’s definition, itself triple: a signifier represents a subject for another signifier. “The” signifier is at least three. And returning to Freud, we read that there are three times to the drive (active, passive, reflexive), that there are two libidos (red, green, the sexual object and the ego), but that it is the same: there is a time when narcissism takes the ego as object (yellow).

All this is nonetheless of importance if one considers that what Lacan calls the cut is essentially the cut of interpretation. Here we are dealing with accounting for the psychoanalytic act, nothing less!

I therefore refer to my other articles so that one can see how these concepts function as closely as possible to the clinic.

### Phenomenological appendix

![](images/0bcd0bfe66f6113cd80ce641451dadcd361ae050d809219b6a3d6e49b7ed09f3.jpg)

The vase with two images is only another writing of the Möbius strip; or the reverse: the Möbius strip is another writing of the vase with two faces. Obviously, the example is too simple, because if one sees two faces and the other a vase, by speaking to each other they will soon see what the other saw.

If the question is more complicated, as in what we are debating, it is the same: it is by speaking to each other that we can bring our points of view closer. I believe that the figure of the vase (ahaha! the figure of the vase! do you hear?) is nevertheless exemplary in this: what is common to the two figures is the line that separates them – this line that operates the switch from one perception to the other. This line, as edge, is a writing of the signifier. By saying “the figure of the vase”, I mean, on one slope: the metaphor represented by this ambiguous experience; and on the second slope, I presentify this ambiguity as such, by making “face” heard as a synonym for “figure”. It is the same word, but it has two different meanings; I can choose one, the other, or leave it in ambiguity – which is why the Möbius strip has three twists.

Thus visual perception: one might think that it is individual, and that afterwards I speak of what I see to the other, who speaks to me of what he sees. The individual is collectivised by borrowing the boots of language, which is common to us. Now language is common to us, certainly, but also in its ambiguity. There is also individual perception of sounds that are supposed to be common. And moreover, we learned to speak at the same time as we learned to see; things had to be shown to us, pointed out to us, so as to link the word and the thing. This learning was at the same time that of ambiguity in all areas.

“This breast is mine,” says the mother, “but I feed you with it”; certainly, the infant does not yet answer, but dear mummy, I formulate in his place, I consider this breast as part of me. The line that separates the two figures is not yet born. This line will be language – a bullfighter who sometimes lets himself be gored by the drive‑bull. That is why I am obliged to imagine their dialogue, but in fact this is indeed what is imagined somewhere in both of them, through the only one who at that moment speaks – the mother. She constructs this line in the imaginary dialogue she weaves with the child. She imagines what he wants, what he asks, what he says to her. She says enough back to him so that he forges a speech first modelled on hers, and which will retain its mark. When I speak, what I imagine I am saying has always been more or less drawn from the vase of another.

Thus all this has been more or less drawn from Lacan’s vase, but I pour it into a sauce of my own making.

By the way, have you noticed that, in a trial, if the lawyer is not believed [cru], his client is cooked [cuit]? [^2]

### Translator’s notes

[^1]: **représentance**: Lacanian term derived from Freud’s *Vorstellungsrepräsentanz* – the representative of the drive in the psyche. Often translated as “representative” or “representance”. Here the author explicitly links it to the function of creating representations.

[^2]: **cru / cuit**: A pun in French: *cru* means “believed” (past participle of *croire*) and also “raw”; *cuit* means “cooked” and also “done for”. The author plays on the trial metaphor: if the lawyer is not believed (not taken as credible), the client is cooked (doomed). This is a typical example of the linguistic play the author uses.
