# The Melun Diptych and the *Acoupure* in the Borromean Knot

*The Virgin and Child Surrounded by Angels* – Jean Fouquet (c. 1452–1458)

It was my friend François Duchenne who submitted this painting to my sagacity, saying that it had greatly impressed him, without his being able to say exactly why. Composed of two panels now separated, it dazzles with the brilliant colours that a faithful restoration has given back to it. Seized by the same astonishment – which I personally call a "diffuse unease" – I set to work on an analysis.

![](images/5f7a65d8b180c5ea7e12a2687e313fbc52cd4f56f2ef7a5d70c8f27a5cc0b3c7.jpg)

Concerning the left-hand panel, which will focus our attention, the following graphic analysis is proposed:

![](images/9bbc21833326bf5b2d071577d4cdbe2fb6088cccce5a115e300c683c25335999.jpg)

It has its pertinences, notably in relation to the symbolism of the period: the ratio of the side to the diameter of a pentagon involves the golden ratio. On the other hand, it is somewhat approximate. For example, while some angles of the pentagons rest on the eye of certain angels, others do not. The triangle of the maternal face, inverting the triangle suggested by the opening of the mantle, is also well observed, all the more so as it is equal and inverse to the preceding one, allowing its extension to coincide with the lower horizontal side of one of the pentagons. Its right side crosses a side of the other pentagon at the level of Jesus' pointing hand. Certainly, but no other crossing indicates a remarkable point. One of them rests on the direction of Jesus' arm pointing, the direction of the Virgin's opposite arm, and the horizontal. The other is justified by the top of the diadem, the Virgin's right wrist, and the eye of two angels. That's not bad, but it leaves the other corners and angles of the pentagons empty of relevant support.

The two inverted pentagons would also be found in the composition of the enamelled medallion bearing Jean Fouquet's self-portrait, which adorned the frame along with other medallions.

The golden ratio is certainly found here, since it is established by the ratio between a side of the pentagon and its diameter, which passes through the painter's eye-line, essential to his art.

![](images/52342d9617b76d05372212e80867fb7e0b5ed31a4416137e5ff0d8f69bc140a3.jpg)

Despite the interest of this well-known symbolism, I remain somewhat unsatisfied. It does not explain the diffuse unease I feel when contemplating the work – and which I know I am not alone in feeling. This analysis highlights a few harmonious proportions, but that is all.

How can we not admit, then, that we are fascinated by that uncovered breast, of an entirely excessive roundness, almost evoking, beyond the hemisphere, the sphere... as if it were an added piece rather than an integral part of the lady. This impression is reinforced by the unusual spacing of the two breasts, as if they were movable, even detachable objects. Would this not evoke the phallic aspect of this feminine attribute? The calm nudity of the infant Jesus does not disillusion us. Eroticism reigns, even if that is precisely what one dares not say, so much does the sacred character of the figure, underlined by the hieratic attitudes, intimidate us. A mother's bared breast is in principle only exposed to bring into play its nourishing function. That is what we are told to justify the attitude: breastfeeding. Yet there is no evocation of that here: the Virgin is not opening her bodice to give the breast, the child does not have his face turned toward the object of his covetousness, and, if it is because he is satisfied, having taken all there was to take, she makes no move to tie herself up again. Under these conditions, what is she doing other than exhibiting herself? The cover of piety shelters many pleasures!

In general, culture readily accepts that a woman bares her chest in the family when it comes to feeding the child. Everyone pretends to find it cute and unproblematic, at the cost of concealing the diffuse unease that seizes each person before the unveiling of this eminently erotic object. Brassens gave a superb version of it in his song:

> When Margot unfastened her bodice  
> To give her cat a drop of milk,  
> All the lads, all the lads of the village  
> Were lalalalalala...

To the point that the listener forgets that the traditional lallation "lalalalala" – the embodiment of song without words – refers not only to the scene itself, but also to the exclamation of the baby who enjoys the object, certainly alimentary, but also enjoyable for him, while what is still impossible for him – to speak – becomes an interdiction for the village lads: what is there, but can only be veiled under the censorship of rediscovered lallation, what is there, by their presence, is the phallus coming to stand before its absence, replaced, metaphorised by the breast. It is there, and a little bit there!

Apparently what the Virgin exhibits is her child, Christ. She opens her arms and holds him in a fold of her mantle. It is true that he is completely naked, but... she is too, in part! She exhibits her phallus in its double form: the breast and the child. Especially since each fold of the said mantle readily lends itself to being read as a female sex: what is supposed to be hidden underneath is unveiled and multiplied on top, in the very fabric of the veil.

The Virgin is seated on a magnificent, richly ornamented throne. The study of the other panel of the diptych reveals vanishing lines that allow us to reconstruct the structure of the perspective, whose point of view is situated in the other part of the diptych, while the vanishing point points to the Virgin's neck. It first comes to mind that this is what tilts her throne. This gives an impression of strangeness, because the first perception imposes it on us as frontal. Yet that is not the case. In the end, it seems that she turns away from the donor, who is the subject of the other panel (normally on the left), in order to devote her gaze exclusively to her son, on the right from our point of view.

![](images/39bebfeba5c962b3eba83bde18437fb28475f8c588df99a76644bb86aa34818d.jpg)

![](images/a2028a63a65af0f3a344840ab95037db91b340e8266b6686cc603e83c51680fb.jpg)

Her inclination causes her body to hide from us one of the black balls set in gold and adorned with pearls that make up the frame of her advent. The supposed symmetry of the throne invites us indeed to imagine these balls as the corners of a quadrilateral, one part of which is hidden from us: one is missing, placing the portrait under the aegis of an absence immediately veiled by the imagination. Like the child discovering the feminine lack and immediately covering it with the phallus, we fill this one on the spot by means of the imagination suggested by symmetry.

Two small pearls placed behind the child's ear would give an indication of a displacement of this missing ball. It would have followed the opening gesture of the Virgin's mantle. In that case, it indeed indicates the role of the child as coming to substitute for the ball that, in any case, is missing from its... expected place. I owe it to Michel Thomé to have pointed out that this might be due to perspective twisting the structure of the work: the Virgin's throne would not face us, but would be inclined according to that perspective coming from the other panel on the left. Thus, the black marble ball at the bottom left would be placed at the end of the armrest and not on the backrest. We can verify this by transferring a circle of the same diameter from the lower ball to the upper one: the latter turns out to be slightly smaller, confirming its distance.

![](images/4ca8019ae7a7f1742e35660d84e54bc29b950f53f1382c7f5a151d32118ac075.jpg)

The left armrest alone would face us, hence this illusion, reinforced by the skirt of golden threads falling from each ball, concealing its footing. The right armrest, shifted by perspective, would therefore be in its proper place behind the child's ear... which nonetheless underlines the shift and the empty place – an effect of perspective, i.e., of a point of view coming from the other panel. All this would determine a second point of view, completely opposed to the first.

Let us draw the perceptual framework suggested to us by the three black balls and the concealment of the footing of the lower ball, leading to the idea of the missing ball at bottom right:

![](images/85c359dc9cda334e401ea842e74a733782795ad3b5bbe97500f36dd70578f9f4.jpg)

Now let us draw the additional, larger framework that takes account of the clue of the ball hidden behind the child's head:

![](images/8f465280dd1649f1983b729fe25ddbecfe8c274bb8aeb5252764a1f6bd97c906.jpg)

By joining the corner of these two frameworks, we obtain the outline of the veiled armrest and thus the lines of a new perspective (in white) and a new vanishing point, specific to this panel, coming to intersect, in opposition to, the geometric perspective of the whole (in yellow). We can further add a perspective we will call "psychoanalytic" (in black), centred on the lack that geometric perspective hollows out without showing it explicitly.

![](images/9c839c6eab2bbce1aaf9c88d43db5d83523a3be383d9b95a8417db0da20947eb.jpg)

![](images/4fc58d260860a17e350286cde6fcca8a483ded0121509bba558e546fafb6d8e5.jpg)

Slightly offset from the place of the lack, on either side of the void, two other balls offer themselves as replacements: the mother's breast and the child's head. This is perhaps the origin of the excessively spherical character of the bared breast and the excessive spacing between the two breasts. The latter in some way doubles the gap between the black balls of the armrests, whose excess is reinforced by its veiling (golden threads veiling the footing, the child's head concealing the 4th ball). In the two black balls on the left, exegetes have noted the reflection of a mullioned window. They read in it an allusion to the common metaphor of the time, comparing Mary's virginity to window glass: light passes through it without breaking it. And the breast, and the child, occult the lack without filling it!

This gives us a little arithmetic in the form of 4 – 1 = 3. Now we observe precisely 2 × 3 red angels + 3 blue angels = 9 angels + 1, Jesus. Or again: 10 – 1 = 9 = 3 × 3. In other words, the dynamic of lack does not affect only the back of the chair. This is a highly dubious arithmetic that I mention here only as a curiosity.

The sexual character of the veiling-unveiling is confirmed by other geometric particularities of the painting. A line joining the child's sex and its doubling – the pointing finger – turns out to be the exact median of his body, passing through his navel, and catching, in its extension, an eye of each of the two red angels situated above. That's still something given to see, all that! A line passing through the sex of the infant Jesus and then through his mother's nipple strikes the exact top of the maternal diadem. It seems to indicate, in the other direction, the place on the child's body where his mother's gaze falls.

![](images/68bab0300df2098dbf8b92d12b532df0bfc7259594182d8c63ae9449b0c56af5.jpg)

But what does the child's finger, so magnificently erected, point to? Quite simply the portrait of the donor, Étienne Chevalier, grand treasurer of King Charles VII, supported by Saint Stephen, his patron saint. In other words, a father figure, since it is indeed the sowing of his gold coins that engendered the work.

![](images/2620ce31993c9b6416f3886ce9f8a1879b93304d3cd34c10fb0b542f72f69ad1.jpg)

![](images/0d072f546f347c1d0619f2e90ff0beb8caecbb5ee983637119e328cabfd52741.jpg)

Saint Stephen holds a book thought to be either the Gospels or perhaps Étienne Chevalier's Book of Hours. On this book, a flintstone recalls that he was stoned. I wondered whether a relation could be established between this flintstone and the empty place of the displaced ball, for both are situated on the same horizontal line. Of roughly the same size, they also have roughly the same shape if the flint is turned vertically. Their distance is equal to that separating the Virgin's neck from Saint Stephen's neck. We recall that the vanishing point of the geometric perspective lies in the middle of the Virgin's neck. The void, the stone that kills: castration and death are of the same size, on the same parallel.

But what is that red angel placed behind the infant Jesus doing? His curiously placed hand, the slight arching of his body, his somewhat bizarre gaze – all end up suggesting the unimaginable, not only within the framework of a religious painting, dedicated to the most absolute respect, but more broadly within the framework of childhood, dedicated to an original purity that no transgression should taint. Yet I have heard transgressive stories featuring babies! Fantasies or reality, both exist and coexist without excluding each other. I therefore draw the line indicating this interpretive direction by connecting what can be guessed of the red angel's hand to the child's sex. It intersects the direction that the latter points at the level of the oblique braid adorning the maternal dress. This braid turns out to be exactly perpendicular to the direction – hard to admit – of a sexual desire of the angel for Jesus. It is parallel to the left opening of the Virgin's arm; if we draw this parallel so that it grazes the back of her hand and the side of the covered breast, it aims at the top of the diadem, where it meets the line joining the child's sex to the mother's nipple. The braid is then exactly median between arm and phallus.

![](images/32d4f48eeb88448a1de4554b05e68c3f4f5f9b4eb8c431ffa2510d97bfb61364.jpg)

So what does the infant Jesus' finger point to? The father, certainly, or at least a father figure. But given the activity of the red angel behind him, is he not trying to point out a culprit? Pure speculation, of course. That is where one would perhaps have to delve into the life of Agnès Sorel (supposed model for the Virgin) or of the painter. But after all, what does it matter to us? Especially since nothing is certain about the identity of the model and of Charles VII's mistress. But there is at least there an indication of a fantasy.

Behind this dubious angel hangs, right at the edge of the painting, a form that could be attributed to a piece of wing, but which is nonetheless quite phallic[^1].

We can then trace a circle whose diameter is the width of the panel, placed at the middle in the height direction. Its horizontal diameter, passing through the centre of one of the marble balls and the area where one is missing, coincides with the base of the framework we had drawn initially. Its vertical diameter falls exactly on the midpoint between the maternal arm and the child's phallus, at the meeting point of the braid and the direction indicated by the finger. Note that this circle is calibrated to the width of the work, which was not the case for the circle used in the pentagonal symbolism.

![](images/0b66f4c06815f26c39b00a863af66eae99aab9170fd206e696f1e7252a0e832c.jpg)

The gesture of the Virgin's right hand holding a fold of her mantle between thumb and forefinger, generating one of the vaginal images, recalls the corresponding hand position of the child, placed on his thigh. At this spot, one can see two folds of the chubby flesh: one, in relief, seems held by the child between thumb and forefinger; the other, right next to it, in hollow. The whole nonetheless ends up evoking a phallus adjacent to a virginal female sex. The mother would hold an image of femininity, the boy an image of virility right next to the threat of castration.

This displacement of the child's sex onto his left thigh may evoke for us the same kind of displacement on the maternal body: in the middle of the braid, at the meeting point of 4 notable lines.

Let us now look at the gazes. Two red angels and one blue one contemplate the Virgin. One red angel, at top right, watches in her direction, but he could just as well be observing the child. The blue one just to his right looks up at something unknown, which is rather strange given his slightly disgusted expression. Would this be the one who understood the sexual aspect of the scene and turns away from it? We had already noted the strange gaze of the red angel placed behind Christ. Above, an angel bows his head: is it out of contrition, to see the infant Jesus, the scene taking place, or to look at the only angel who looks at us, with a particularly bewildered air, and who consequently questions our own gaze?

![](images/9a7395ec964047b89a8e48c87a8c0c07f1ab2ee0a0cd65fd0ec4e2b009dac67f.jpg)

This questioning sends us back to everything I have just unveiled. Do we accept seeing it or not? Are we shocked or not? And therefore: who are we? "What is your desire?" – he seems to be saying to us.

A small topological formalisation can provide us with a universal model of what is written there, which has the name: the process of repression.

## Span surface of the Borromean knot

This is an attempt to find a correspondence between the theory of surfaces – notably the Möbius strip – and that of knots, by means of the span surface.

Here is the crossing obtained by twisting a strip of paper, i.e. the inversion of the y dimension into –y, and the inversion of the z dimension into –z (third dimension, above-below):

![](images/769edc7b584a8fd4bd8f0bbf3009abd31d7d4d7ac036b297a3e9e5e1e606bfd3.jpg)

We note that the top edge goes to the bottom while the bottom edge goes to the top, while passing underneath the former, exactly as if it were a crossing of strings. At the same time, the twisting operation has brought out the underside of the sheet of paper. It is the other side, that of which, usually, I want to know nothing, possibly attributing it to the other. It is the slip, the symptom, the dream, the hallucination: in short, a formation of the unconscious manifesting itself in this form of return of the repressed. For the subject, of course, always finds himself in this position of reader, always reading only one side, the other having to be turned over to appear to him, first in this manifest form, then, by means of another twist, in its latent form through interpretation.

We can immediately present a version schematising our attitude before the Melun Diptych. The contemplation of the painting offers us at the same time its manifest aspect and this feeling of diffuse unease that pushes toward interpretation. The latter, as we see, turns out to be incompatible, in its now explicit aspect, with the manifest aspect initially experienced. One would have to join the last "above" to the first, but an above cannot be the other side of something already above. The repressive force finds its reason there: one would have to keep the sexual interpretation below, but interpretation brings it back above. As on the Möbius strip, we must therefore preserve a zone of indecision where opposites coexist, after having shifted the meanings of the surfaces:

![](images/bcd97200f0b4de37b3712ce0840e3a747a4e7a001886b1cc6881acd6d0a10f38.jpg)

![](images/d802e38915816986af7b495675328d95a643e6af11753140debe02b9a94a35c7.jpg)

This is therefore an *après-coup* reconstruction, writing the analysis of repression and its interpretation. Through interpretation, we have understood that the diffuse unease was due to this coexistence of a holy and respectable image with a sexual exhibition, as incompatible with each other as the above and below of a surface. It is their contradiction that is perceived as a slight unease, because both are perceived at the same time, even if the second is repressed. The Möbius strip appears here in its double structural aspect: as cut, on the left, between above and below; and as surface, on the right, where the cut remains inefficient (above and below are not separated).

What allows the passages from one side to the other – these inversions – is the hole of the third dimension, the one around the sheet of paper. This latter is not in the writing. It is nevertheless symbolised by a void in the writing, by a blank outside the edges, and by the twist – places where the edges, crossing, allow one to read on one side the above, on the other the below. This authorises us to read this figure as a metaphor of castration: the hole is outside the writing, real; it ex‑sists, but it is not as such in the writing, where it nonetheless appears in a symbolised state. The interruption of the line passing underneath, in the centre, is the most readable "writing" of it, precisely because at that point, writing is interrupted by a void: one cannot write what ex‑sists to writing, the real of the hole. There, it ceases to be written, which, paradoxically, allows it to cease not to be written. This is why what is impossible – the real presence of the hole in writing – returns by an interruption of the latter: an inter‑dict.

## Let us apply this to the writing of the Borromean knot

We posit the following axiom: the zones delimited by the contour of the lines representing the strings will be systematically alternated into surfaces and holes. Thus we take account of Lacan's later definitions, making the hole the Symbolic, the surface the Imaginary, and the Real what ex‑sists to representation, and is therefore not represented in the drawing. The crossings of strings will be considered as twists of the surface, i.e., the place where we pass from above to below – an operation authorised by the hole.

We alternate surface and hole:

![](images/8358e044b9bb7ef1d016d9bba5a1ea8f7ee8ce7d691f26cab349f46d3368e2f8.jpg)

So far no problem. Now let us consider the crossings as twists, which obliges us to orient the surfaces; on either side of a twist we must find here an above (green) and there a below (red). We see immediately that we cannot go very far in orientation, for logical conflicts arise at once, whatever order we follow:

![](images/3b7a5068356ad41e430c9a2147e1120818b086d1c74230e3d4b438c5c197efad.jpg)

![](images/70b44ebb8a1dcc3b4132e24b22faa8048430501bf591716a2d8fe37a23aee1da.jpg)

Starting from the left zone, which we decide to be "above" (green), and crossing a first twist toward the centre, we must therefore name the next zone "below" and colour it red. Two possibilities then present themselves: continue upward or downward. In both cases, and in the direction of our travel, we should again note "above" and colour these two zones green. But this is forbidden, for then we would have a green zone above and below opposing the green zone on the left from which we started: from the point of view of that zone, and if we had started upward and downward instead of toward the centre, these two zones would have to be "below" and therefore red:

![](images/c7be02291dd9836d119c9367df5bb76852d8e43be9f0a785df26dc20b01d4816.jpg)

![](images/f3289824458875617276d7b49fafeac2426c5db5357d284e946c3072ba930828.jpg)

Being both red and green, both above and below, we are forced not to choose and to leave these zones in their original colour "yellow":

![](images/74482ad5895661e78c55d235ff09e9f9dd4eeb68005a51610c15ceef8beefacf.jpg)

![](images/32ed8ae2ce0c8249f40a116b793c2bb4064eaf829ced6a9986aed6a7b90dcdc0.jpg)

Here is an excellent image of the logic of the psychic apparatus: meeting a contradiction (above = below), conscious judgment stops and allows the two options to subsist in the unconscious. The indeterminacy of these two zones then joins the original indeterminacy, where all zones were yellow. This is how we can call this original indeterminacy, with Freud, primal repression, and with Lacan, the Real. It is therefore *après‑coup*, after the functioning of the Symbolic (the hole) and the Imaginary (the surface), that we can say: there remain disoriented zones, of which we can say nothing, which we name by default "the Real", extending by logical consequence this naming to the prior indeterminacy. In the functioning of the unconscious, primal repression draws to itself repression proper; it is part of the forces that nourish repression. In our model, we indeed see how two of the zones originally coloured yellow by their indeterminacy return to yellow after our attempt at orientation. We recognise a remarkable homology of functioning that makes, on the one hand, repression proper and primal repression coincide, and on the other, zones that admit the contradiction above‑below and zones for which there was neither above nor below. We therefore cannot differentiate between the two parts of Gödel's theorem: the undecidable (neither…nor…, primal repression) and contradiction (both…and…, repression proper).

Let us once again consider this as a theorisation of our attitude before the Melun Diptych. At first sight, we see a representation of the Virgin and Child: that is a manifest writing, green. But we cannot shake off a certain unease: so there is something latent, red. This latent implies an interpretation, which should bring back above the sexual meaning glimpsed. That is where it encounters a contradiction: a sexual exhibition cannot be compatible either with the religious icon of the Virgin, or with the universal figure of the mother, which were our starting vision.

![](images/9b43a3e79aaa38fa9328be0a77c70d594ee740959314606984a30a3027733c98.jpg)

![](images/fa0954853398af04398694c028c893ab2bd6e721ac06e561b77112108cf32a86.jpg)

We find ourselves before the same structure as that of the Möbius strip introduced above, except that this time we note the presence of two zones of indeterminacy.

Let us now see if we can refer to Lacan's concepts. Here is the way he organises his concepts on the figure of the Borromean knot:

![](images/f3dbb391d553ee58b814fa5c3d7d78e382e917c095c9c93ca409154d8bb33762.jpg)

I have added P, the Name‑of‑the‑Father, in the exterior hole. Lacan does not do so, but it seemed appropriate to me. Lacan did not organise his writing in surfaces and holes. He simply places his concepts in the spaces offered. He places the *objet a* at the centre because this concept is central to his theory. Sense and the two jouissances – phallic jouissance J(Φ) and the jouissance of the Other J(A) – revolve around it, like planets around the sun.

Let us establish the relation with our topological experiment. In Lacan's theory, the concept of *objet a* represents what remains unorientable in practice, i.e., impossible to represent. That is why this object is cause of desire: because it can in no way be attained. It will always be missing in the field of representation. It causes the desire to orient oneself, i.e., to take possession of those zones where all possession proves impossible. Thus, while remaining unorientable, it orients. More precisely, it was impossible at the start, before the symbolic machine was set to work. Afterwards, it becomes forbidden, because the symbolic machine has set conventions that can no longer be rejected. Thus the prohibition is projected *après‑coup* onto the impossibility of origins, just as in the Oedipus complex, whose law prohibiting the mother projects onto the "before" the primal loss, impossible to recover, of the Thing, at the moment of entry into the law of language. In my writings, then, *objet a* can only be situated in those yellow zones that remain disoriented.

We can then note that, topologically, our three rings are strictly identical, in consistency, place and function. They could not therefore represent the Real, the Imaginary and the Symbolic, for that would mean that the Real would have no difference from the Symbolic, nor from the Imaginary.

In my view, this is what Lacan confusedly perceived when he proposed, in *Le Sinthome*, his new definition: the Symbolic is the hole, the Imaginary is the surface, the Real is what ex‑sists. I believe he did not have time to realise the scope of his new definitions, which call all his previous writings into question. Indeed, until then he had only been plastering concepts onto drawings, which gives what I will call schemas. With this taking into account of the dialectic of surfaces and holes, we move beyond schemas to arrive at a writing of concepts, in which the very structure of writing – topological – writes what the concept describes. Thus, as we have just seen, I do not place the *objet a* necessarily at the centre because it is central to the theory, but because its topological place (*topos‑logos*: discourse on places) is deduced from our effort to orient the figure, confining it to zones impossible to orient. Thus topological writing approaches Chinese writing, which more or less describes the concept it represents (thing‑representation) rather than the sound with which it is spoken (word‑representation). It is a letter and not the writing of a signifier.

We have just encountered roughly the same problematic between the "pentagonal" interpretation of the Melun Diptych and the one I proposed afterwards. The first is steeped in references to an ancient symbolism – the relations of the pentagon to the golden ratio, i.e., a knowledge – while the second starts from the experience of unease felt in contemplating the work, following an interpretative path organised both by the evident nudity and by much more hidden but readable lines of force. This is the whole difference between an interpretative discourse resting on a prior knowledge – symbolism – and a writing that, by its internal contradiction (above = below), describes the unease experienced in a perceptual experience – i.e., the effect of the Symbolic. Brought back to the topological play of surfaces and holes, symbolism spreads out a surface (signified, meaning) while the Symbolic opens holes (sense). Symbolism is here an equivalent of the Imaginary, opposing the Symbolic as surface to hole.

Jouissance being the possession of an object, it cannot be inscribed in a hole, which is a lack of surface. On the other hand, it can be inscribed in disoriented zones, which are a lack of hole. Unfortunately we now have only one disoriented zone left, the other having already been identified as the place of the *objet a*. Let us grant it the status of J(A), jouissance of the Other. However, it must be noted that, from a topological point of view, nothing allows us to distinguish these two unorientable zones. We are forced to fall back into the arbitrariness of naming, which could just as easily make us invert the name of one zone onto the other. On the other hand, what about phallic jouissance? We could say from this that the model does not fit, and that we must therefore find another. Or else, that it requires questioning the concept. It is this latter option that I have chosen.

The phallus is defined by its absence: it only takes on meaning at the moment when the child, having perceived the difference of the sexes, attributes a cause to it: castration. The phallus does not exist before, nor beyond. In this sense, the concept of phallic jouissance seems to me self‑contradictory: one never enjoys the phallus, always absent somewhere, just as one never enjoys the object, always already lost – which always falls under the meaning of the jouissance of the Other, i.e., that which one imagines the Other enjoys. But if there were jouissance, there would be no lack, hence no desire. Jouissance is a fantasmatic position, for psychic reality is founded on lack[^2]. In our model this lack is represented by holes and is threefold:

- the phallus, because it can only posit itself as such through castration. This is how Lacan conceives the symbolic phallus, which one might more accurately call castration, it seems to me. But let us be content with the letter Φ.

- the Name‑of‑the‑Father, because it conceptualises the absence of the father as such. Bearer of the phallus or agent of castration, he is not the father of reality, but the function introducing the separation between mother and child, between thing and representation.

- sense, which means nothing but is constituted by the "I say" as such – the act of speaking – which orients the subject in his relation to the other, whatever signifieds and meanings are unveiled. It also depends on the hole between the thing and its representation.

Jouissance remains a myth, which our model faithfully transcribes: a disoriented, unorientable zone remains from a lost paradise. The phallus gives the reason for it, every loss being henceforth reduced to the measure of castration. This is what Freud already said of partial objects – the oral and the anal: they are constituted as lost only in the *après‑coup* of castration. In our writing, a hole seems most appropriate to account for this loss under the title of a function – the symbolic function, otherwise named the phallic function. But it must be written simply as Φ, and no longer as J(Φ). Is there, moreover, a phallic jouissance? This discussion can continue at the conceptual level, and I will not settle it here (sic). But at the level of our topological writing, the question is settled: if there is a jouissance, it must be placed in the unorientable zones, even if we are missing one of them.

Now, let us understand that there is another way of understanding the jouissance of the Other: by pronouncing it "jouissance Other" – that of woman, that which does not exist. By accepting a corollary to the concept of jouissance, in which we add nothing to the list of objects, we obtain a paradoxical jouissance – that of possessing an absence. I indeed find this in practice with those women who find their pleasure (but is it really a jouissance?) in the castration of their partner. It is even quite universal, in the order of a revenge against the supposed possessors of the phallus. For her, the jouissance of the Other is that of the one who enjoys the phallus, and it is that which must be cut. At the extreme, it becomes an adoration of nothing as such. I knew an analysand who told me she particularly appreciated being on a large empty esplanade, where she regularly went to recharge. Another appreciated in modern painting the works of a certain artist who presented almost completely white canvases. We find this passion in the character of the gallery owner played by Isabelle Huppert in *My Worst Nightmare*, Anne Fontaine's film[^3]. Her worst nightmare is indeed the phallus, presented by that man played by Benoît Poelvoorde – a enjoyer who likes to drink, eat and fuck. Conversely, she likes works where there is nothing to see, she hardly eats, does not drink and does not fuck. Finally, we have the model of Saint Teresa of Ávila, whose phallic concern – represented by the angel's arrow – only functions insofar as it remains purely fantasmatic, placed under the aegis of that pure absence which is God.

![](images/ff1a3bc6c8a848066cfe4dc4221cb437a4edc4001911d70a12eae11f59736d28.jpg)

This void, called "jouissance Other", could be placed in a hole of our topological writing, just like the phallus, because it only takes on meaning through the eviction of the representative of the organ, and in this respect it is quite analogous to the concept of the phallus as such. But one could also say it is a surface where what makes void is the absence of orientation. It does not do much to cross the step from one meaning to the other. I will not settle this either, deeming these bases sufficient for our conceptual orientation. The essential thing is to have shown how the support of topology can allow us to question concepts. Only an analysis of the practice of psychoanalysis could give us some other elements to advance in this debate.

![](images/132bb611ac1057d90f2bd1d0fac650d0894725f84abdd400dd48fef3395a885a.jpg)

![](images/ad0c7f2f61799b3ae8903bf18aabf8098360bca0b0a7b4f1ab344bcb132ff100.jpg)

We can then develop the model with a complementary algorithm that will allow us to show how the subject divides, accepting the compromise of a double possibility in order to overcome contradictions. Each twist that places us before a contradiction will lead us to attempt to cut the disputed zone in two: one part will remain conforming to the logic of above‑below, at the cost of repression of the other part.

![](images/48dc01c77713315a2c2bbefe2c3deba3998d9ea91517a41e7ec47d29f74abc8d.jpg)

![](images/9c8bc07001d23e46a479ada5cb6debf16754674670f43c1c644ffb8913ffed89.jpg)

Is this compromise acceptable? On the one hand, it is necessary; on the other, we see taking shape, in the left‑hand writing, the dotted beginning of a new circle – that of the cut – which does not close. We encounter here the Freudian theory of the death drive, Lacanian of the Symbolic, in its topological translation: this circle calls for its closure, the cut calls for its re‑crossing, in order to construct a hole. This opening is the trace in writing of primal repression. No surface testifies to it any longer, but this absence of closure does. It is no longer a symbolic hole, conforming to the definition we adopted at the outset, but a cut that does not close – the *acoupure*. This absence of re‑crossing prevents making a hole. We cannot therefore call it a hole, as Lacan did when presenting his Schema I as the effect of a hole in the Symbolic seconded by a hole in the Imaginary, but we can call it the trace of the Real in writing. Lacan's later definition of the Symbolic as hole, which I promote here, is evidently in contradiction with that one. We could reconcile his two definitions by distinguishing the Real hole from the symbolic hole: the former would represent the incompleteness of the unfinished cut – the *acoupure* – while the latter accepts the loss of surface represented by the cut that, by re‑crossing, makes a hole.

In the right‑hand writing, it is a little more complicated: we have two beginnings of circles of cut that do not re‑cross. Can we invent an algorithm that would allow us both to bring these two cases together and to close the circle of the cut? Yes, by imagining turning each ring around the other two, which form the axis of rotation. I have already developed this algorithm in numerous articles, the clearest of which is probably "Structure of the Borromean knot"[^4]. I propose to consider these displacements, which require passage into the third dimension, as a metaphor for the passage from writing (memory) to speech, which makes a hole in the surface, and then from speech to writing, which orients the surface. Only speech, indeed, is situated in the third dimension, insofar as it is a temporal dimension of the enunciative process. By applying this algorithm six times – i.e., by turning each ring successively in their order, twice – we manage to make the cut between green and red re‑cross in each zone. On the other hand, two zones appear again where the cut does not pass; we must leave them in the yellow of indeterminacy, which writes the presence of primal repression in the structure. This gives the following:

![](images/328b26856dd57f6f9ebbcb6a6ca3c5f96f3bea97c4af6e1d6c76d014320022ef.jpg)

This can also be read as a dialectic between perception and representation, "reality" (i.e., the Real) and memory of reality. Indeed, the algorithm plays on this ambiguity between a real knot whose rings are turned – a movement that cannot be represented – and the writing of the stages, Imaginary (oriented surface) and Symbolic (hole). The yellow zones thus represent, by their disorientation ("impossible to orient"), the impossibility of restoring the Real in its representation.

As in the preliminary stages, we note that the proposed algorithm diverges from Lacan's choice of placing his *objet a* in a single zone at the centre. The topological practice of writing the knot obliges us to this approach, once again resulting in two unorientable zones.
